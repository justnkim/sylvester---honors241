Build 3 · Final

Deliberation in Exercise: Epaminondas and Theanor's Gold

Initially, I argued that Epaminondas’s refusal of Theanor’s gold required little conscious effort because of the years of habituation making the choice inevitable, characterizing the moment of refusal as a performance. However, a closer rereading emphasizes the opposite. Plutarch separates refraining from “base and unlawful pleasures,” an “activity and demonstration” of self-control, from exercise, where someone “voluntarily and repeatedly” distances himself from “perfectly just and permissible” profit (98–99). Since Theanor’s gift is honorable and permitted, Epaminondas’s refusal exhibits exercise in progress; he even asks Theanor to allow the brothers to continue training in poverty (99). Epaminondas does not train himself to replace thought with habit; rather, he works to ensure his reasoning can operate without internal interferences from desires like greed or ambition that discipline must “cut…down by restraints and inhibitions” (98). Epaminondas builds his habit out of choices made freely after thought, indicating his decisions come from deliberation, the weighing of several possible courses of action that get reasoned through over time (99). On my Build 1 reading, habit worked like physical causation, producing the refusal automatically; however, Plutarch’s account keeps open a space where the mind entertains what it could do and what it will not. Deliberation depends on freedom from desire’s interference, and, against modern intuition, that freedom depends on discipline.

What does undisciplined desire do to thought, though? Epaminondas illustrates how dependence on desire ruins clear thought, telling Theanor, “when one is tempted by intense pleasures… one cannot keep one’s thoughts quiet unless one has frequently spurned permitted enjoyments” (99). The danger lies in permitted gain: love of gain “educated to take profit without restraint where it is legitimate” will “wax big at the prospect of crime” (99). Desire scrambles the mental space where the mind compares options; a mind whose thoughts are thrown “into confusion” will never hold two scenarios side by side long enough to weigh them accurately with reason (99). Therefore, discipline protects the conditions of deliberation, suggesting that habituation abolishes inner turmoil, not thought itself. If training protects deliberation, then perhaps the training itself will stage it. Epaminondas describes the Pythagorean exercise of sitting at a table “laden with every kind of food” before giving up all of the goods to servants; the feast provides a realistic, permissible option in front of the mind, and the mind must choose against it (98). Reason and habit work hand in hand according to Epaminondas: exercise “let[s] reason chastise” adventitious desires, and habit gives reason a body of practiced options to pick from with the counterfactuals already at hand (98). Reason judges and commands, whereas habit comes from repeated exercise. However, habit can work against reason. Adventitious desires that “acquire force and strength as a result of long habit under bad education” become “a more powerful depressive influence on the soul” (98). Good training makes desire responsive to reason; it does not replace reason.

If Epaminondas trains to deliberate, why does his speech end by promising that the trained man will “pursue the right without effort” (99)? Exercise unfolds slowly and deliberately, practiced on permitted goods, while activity moves swiftly against unlawful ones. “Effort” here means struggle against desire, not the presence of thought: because his love of gain will not “rise in revolt,” the trained man meets temptation “clear in conscience” (99). My Build 1 reading was right that the decisive moment looks effortless, but wrong about the reason. That ease is the result of deliberation completed earlier.

Does deliberation completed in advance leave a man too slow when action is needed? Theocritus voices this concern early, contrasting Epaminondas with Charon, who has “no special education” yet “volunteers to run great risks for his country” (85). One could perceive deliberation as hesitation in Epaminondas’s case, since he “shows no keenness or enthusiasm” for the revolt despite his extensive education, and the brave Charon will prove more useful to the city of Thebes (85). Yet Caphisias’s reply reveals that Epaminondas’s deliberation has already occurred. He has already tried to argue the conspirators into dropping the plan and, because he failed to do so, now declines to join, citing his disapproval of their actions, even though they all share the same goal. No one would “compel a doctor to operate or cauterize, if he promises to cure the illness without knife or fire” (85). Just like the doctor, Epaminondas weighs two routes to the same cure, choosing the gentler one; he would “join enthusiastically” in any liberation without bloodshed (85–86). Theocritus mistakes this weighing for hesitation, but since the deliberation took place earlier, Epaminondas remains free of doubt when the crisis arrives.

Good training makes desire responsive to reason; it does not replace reason. Build 3

Plutarch, then, places deliberation in exercise, the voluntary refusal of permitted goods, so that virtuous action can later proceed without struggle. He does so to answer Theocritus’s charge that philosophical education leads to passivity: Epaminondas’s calm signals thought already finished, instead of avoided.

Build 4 · Final

The Daemon as Finished Deliberation

In Build 3, I argued that Epaminondas refuses even honorable gifts as a form of training, a requirement to deliberate effectively when the time comes. Our course so far defines deliberation as the weighing of possible scenarios, including counterfactual ones, that do not "automatically ensue as in physical causation" but are reasoned through "over time." But one might object that deliberation does not fit Plutarch's conception of consciousness because the daimon, not reason, appears to make the decisive choices. Socrates never explains his turns; he says only that "the daimonion had occurred" (91). Simmias praises the soul that "yields to the guide" (104), and Timarchus's myth goes further: the best souls are "responsive and obedient to the daimon from the start" (110), and those who see rightly know it is "outside them" (109). The ideal soul, it seems, obeys rather than weighs. Theanor recognizes in Epaminondas "the same daimon" that guided his teacher Lysis (100), that is, the same kind of guidance. If so, Epaminondas's training prepares him to be steered, not to deliberate. I will argue the reverse: in Plutarch, the daimon is what completes deliberation by acting as reason after earthly desires have been subdued.

The objection assumes the sign replaces thought, but its clearest story shows thought intervening. Socrates does not simply swerve: he "stopped and was silent for some time, concentrating on his own thoughts" (91), and only then turned off a street soon overrun by pigs. Nothing in his thoughts could have told him about the pigs, and I cannot explain that sudden onset of knowledge. But the pause shows the sign entering his deliberation as something to weigh rather than a push that bypasses it, meaning Socrates considers the option provided by the daimon instead of following the thought blindly.

Simmias distinguishes two ways a soul can be moved: speech strikes the mind as "a sort of blow," while a higher mind guides "by a mere thought, for it needs no blow" (104). For Plutarch, then, a free consciousness is not one moved by nothing; it is one moved by a thought rather than a push. Yet a thought can win only if the pushes have weakened, and that is exactly what training does. Exercise lets reason "cut... down" unnecessary desires and draw off some of the strength of the rest (98). Once desire no longer shoves, the pause in which two options are weighed becomes a real opening, and a thought can decide.

Acting rightly, then, begins with seeing clearly. Build 4

This is what Epaminondas means when he promises that the trained man will "pursue the right without effort" (99): his desire for gain no longer rises up to cloud his thinking. In Build 1, I read this ease as habituation replacing thought, but, when pressed to join the coup, Epaminondas explains his refusal point by point (112–113), so his ease comes after reasoning, not instead of it. Why, then, would finished reasoning feel like a guide from outside? Timarchus's myth says each soul's purest part, its intellect, floats outside the body, and that those who see rightly "call it [the] daimon" (109). The daimon is the soul's own conscious reason, felt as external because part of it exists outside of our flesh. So, the soul that "yields to the guide" (104) is not obeying something else: if the guide acts as its own reason, yielding just means acting on its own conclusion without resistance.

This raises my own question: does a heightened consciousness see more objectively? Desire distorts as much as it pushes, clouding thought. Epaminondas refuses Theanor’s gold not by suppressing a wish but by seeing the gift as “medicine to friends who are not ill” (97). Acting rightly, then, begins with seeing clearly.

At first, the daimon seemed to undo my claim. However, if one considers how Plutarch uses deliberation to separate being moved by a blow from being moved by a thought, one can begin to see how deliberation might allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality to influence its course. Simmias describes the body as heavy and inert until the soul conceives an idea, at which point "the whole mass rises up" (105). Desire and accident move us the way one object pushes another. Several scenarios stay open only because that chain can be interrupted, and deliberation weighs them. On Plutarch's account, the moments when we feel most torn may be the moments when desire pushes hardest, and Epaminondas's calm shows what it looks like when thought, not force, steers.

Build 5 · Final

Thoughts That Can Mislead: Plutarch and Milton

In previous builds, I argued that Epaminondas trains himself so that thought decides his actions; Milton's Paradise Lost puts the same quality of consciousness, deliberation, through the opposite extreme, a temptation that wins. Plutarch and Milton share two principles. First, deliberation should be moved by thought rather than force: Simmias's higher mind guides "by a mere thought, for it needs no blow" (104), and Adam insists that "what obeys / Reason is free" (IX.351–52). Second, deliberation answers to something beyond itself: Simmias praises the soul that "yields to the guide" (104), while Milton's narrator calls the serpent Eve's "guide" (IX.646). They raise a question Plutarch's account of training leaves open: what if the guide a mind yields to is false?

For Plutarch, error comes from disturbances. Untrained love of gain "will wax big at the prospect of crime" (99), so Epaminondas practices refusing permitted goods because "one cannot keep one's thoughts quiet unless one has frequently spurned permitted enjoyments" (99). Once desire is quiet, judgment follows, and the trained man will "pursue the right without effort" (99). Even the daimon, in Galaxidorus's account, settles only "obscure or inscrutable matters" (92): questions reason cannot decide instead of the ones that it gets wrong.

Milton's tempter reveals the limit of Simmias's contrast. Simmias would classify speech as a “blow” (104), but what moves Eve is the case that speech makes. First Satan unsettles how she sees herself, dismissing Adam as "one man" (IX.545). As Hegel said, self-consciousness "exists only by being recognized" (§178), and the serpent offers Eve a new recognition, and her deliberation begins as she sees herself through his eyes. What persuades her is apparent evidence, a beast that speaks but falsely credits the fruit (IX.598–601). She still sees the command, but now as evidence rather than as something to obey, since God's "forbidding / Commends thee more" (IX.753–54). Being moved by thoughts is not enough for our definition of freedom, because thoughts can be counterfeit.

The capacity that makes a mind free is the same capacity that makes it able to be wrong. Build 5

The difference in principle is what vigilance guards against. Epaminondas's practice guards against desire; Milton's vigilance guards against false reasons. Reason was "made right," Adam says, yet must be bid "well beware and still erect" (IX.352–53). Eve lapses when she calls the serpent an "author unsuspect" (IX.771) and closes the question of her source. Because her will stays free (IX.351) and the warning was given, the lapse is hers.

Beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that the capacity that makes a mind free is the same capacity that makes it able to be wrong. A push can be strong or weak, but never mistaken or at fault, so it cannot be free, like a rock falling from gravity, for example. Plutarch implies part of this: souls that must "listen" and "heed" (112) can fail too, though he leaves open what happens when the voice itself is false. This revises my Build 4 claim that the daimon is finished deliberation. Deliberation is never finished; rather, it is a dialectical process that grows by overcoming what opposes it.

Plutarch and Milton both use deliberation to separate the effects of blows and thoughts. But how do we get from deliberation to allowing consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality, influencing its course? Deliberate responses track what something is taken to mean, not its physical properties. The same command first stops Eve (IX.651), then draws her once forbidding "commends" the fruit (IX.754): one command, opposite actions, because her reading of it has changed. A brain-level description can record that her state changed, but not whether she had reason to change it. A push acts only from what exists; Eve's deliberation answers to a counterfactual, in this case the death that has never happened, so her thought of it exists now, but what would make that thought right or wrong does not. She fails by vouching for her source; only a mind that can consciously take up its own reasons, and ask whether they are true, can do that. Deliberation intervenes not by force, but by making action answerable to what a reason means and whether it is true. Simmias marvels that "the mere entertaining of an idea moves the body so effortlessly" (105): the idea supplies no force of its own, only the reason the body moves this way rather than that. The freedom this secures is answerability to reasons: action not fixed by force alone. This does not yet show that consciousness acts apart from the brain, so the open question here is whether what a thought means helps cause action, or only accompanies it.

Build 5 · Draft

In Builds 3 and 4, I argued that Plutarch's Epaminondas trains himself so that a thought, not desire, can decide his actions. Milton's Paradise Lost tests the same quality of consciousness, deliberation, against the most extreme case: a temptation that wins. Both authors insist that virtue means nothing until it faces a real alternative. The Pythagoreans sit before a table "laden with every kind of food" only to give it away (98), and Eve asks, "what is faith, love, virtue unassayed, / Alone, without exterior help sustained?" (IX.335–36). Both tie freedom to reason, as Adam does when he says "what obeys / Reason is free" (IX.351–52). Both show desire wrecking the calm that deliberation needs: Plutarch's untrained man cannot keep his thoughts from "confusion" (99), and after the Fall, passion shakes the couple's "inward state of mind, calm region once" (IX.1124). And both stage deliberation beside a guide: Socrates' daimon, and the serpent, whom the narrator calls Eve's "guide" (IX.646).

The differences lie in what each author thinks the test should be run on. Plutarch separates exercise, refusing permitted goods, from activity, refusing unlawful ones (98–99). Epaminondas practices on Theanor's honorable gift so that he will be ready to refuse unlawful gain. Eve reverses the order, and she seeks her trial alone, on the one forbidden thing, with no practice behind her. Adam warns her, "Seek not temptation… trial will come unsought" (IX.364–66), and afterward draws the lesson: "Let none henceforth seek needless cause t' approve / The faith they owe" (IX.1140–41). However, Milton does not reject testing itself; Adam lets her go because "thy stay, not free, absents thee more" (IX.372): virtue that is forced is not virtue. Milton and Plutarch agree that the soul must be tested; they disagree about whether the first test should be the most dangerous one.

The deeper difference is when deliberation happens, and through what eyes. Epaminondas has deliberated long before the gold arrives. Eve deliberates in the moment, and her deliberation is real: she weighs knowledge against death, God's motives against the serpent's evidence (IX.745–79). But she weighs a scene desire has already painted. The fruit is "Fair to the eye, inviting to the taste" (IX.777), exactly the "fair appearing good" Adam warned could make reason "dictate false" (IX.354–55). She even turns freedom into an argument for eating: "what profits then / Our inward freedom?" (IX.761–62). Even as she eats, the narrator cannot say whether her delight is "true / Or fancied so" (IX.788–89). Satan shows the same failure from the other side, considering repentance carefully (IV.93–97), but "Disdain forbids me" (IV.82) before the weighing begins, and his deliberation ends in "Evil, be thou my good" (IV.110).

This is also why the two guides differ, since the serpent offers his "conduct" (IX.630) and promises the very thing Eve lacks, clear sight: "your eyes, that seem so clear / Yet are but dim, shall perfectly be then / Opened and cleared" (IX.706–8). Instead, Adam and Eve find "their eyes how opened and their minds / How darkened" (IX.1053–54). Plutarch's daimon guides only souls already trained to hear it while Milton shows what happens when an untrained soul chooses its guide by appearances.

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that deliberation is only as free as the soul's sight is clear. In Build 4, I argued that desire threatens deliberation by pushing. Milton shows that desire also paints: Eve reasons carefully and still chooses wrongly, because the option she weighs is not what it seems. The course defines deliberation as weighing scenarios over time, but both authors suggest the scenarios must first be seen truly, and that this sight is earned. Epaminondas refuses Theanor's gold because he sees it for what it is, "medicine to friends who are not ill" (97). Milton's remedy, after the Fall, is Plutarch's: Michael tells Adam to "add / Deeds to thy knowledge… temperance" (XII.581–83), promising "a paradise within thee" (XII.587). Freedom, then, is not only being moved by a thought rather than a push. It is being moved by a true thought, and only training teaches a soul to tell a true thought from a fair-appearing one.

Build 6 · Draft (contribution to the team conclusion)

Why would anyone refuse gold they could honorably accept? We found that, for Plutarch, Epaminondas refuses the gift to train: by spurning permitted pleasures, he quiets the desires that would otherwise push him, so that a thought rather than a force decides his actions. However, if a thought can move us, then a thought can also mislead us. Milton’s serpent persuades Eve not by force but by argument: he flatters her, offers false evidence, and turns God’s own prohibition into a reason to eat. Freedom and error, we concluded, are one capacity. Freedom and error may then be one capacity, so the act of deliberation is never truly finished.
