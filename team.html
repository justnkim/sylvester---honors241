Team Introduction

ThesisThree possible tests for consciousness: where a thought comes from, how a mind interprets it, and what it makes the body do.

At first, connecting Plutarch’s Socrates’ Daimonion, with its discussion of divine voices, souls, and signs, to Westworld’s artificial minds and programmed hosts seems improbable. Yet both of these texts raise a problem that we have returned to in our discussions of consciousness which is where do our thoughts come from, and does their origin actually determine if we are free to act upon them? From Jaynes’s distinction between voices that are understood as either gods or the self to discussing determinism in lecture and whether consciousness truly can cause physical action, the line between external influence and internal thought becomes harder to draw. Our Plutarch reading uses this sort of ambiguity to identify whether consciousness depends on the source of a thought, our ability to interpret it, its power to in turn produce action, or some sort of combination of the three.

Test 1 · SourceA voice reaches the mind without sound. The hosts' voices come from code. Does the origin matter?

In Plutarch’s Socrates’ Daimonion, Simmias describes Socrates’ daimonion not as an actual vision, but as a voice that communicates with his mind. Ordinary speech must physically find us through sound, while this communication seems to go around the senses altogether. So, Plutarch complicates where consciousness actually comes from: is the mind just attempting to respond to physical inputs, or can it receive and act on something beyond those inputs? Westworld highlights this distinction and complicates it further because the hosts also hear voices inside their minds, but theirs originate in programming. As we watch hosts begin to claim these programmed voices as their own thought, Plutarch gives us even another way to question what consciousness actually requires. Perhaps where a thought comes from matters less than whether a mind can interpret it and both matter less than what we choose to do with said thought. Following this idea that use matters more than origin, Plutarch's account of thought moving the body offers a concrete test for what 'doing something' with a thought actually looks like.

Test 3 · Action“Thought contacts thought”: consciousness is what turns an inner thought into motion.

Furthermore, Plutarch explores how consciousness can receive thoughts from an outside source using the senses, which he refers to as “thought contacts thought.” Within Westworld, we can apply Plutarch’s idea of consciousness to understand how the hosts develop beyond simply following their programmed algorithms. Using this concept, the hosts count as conscious when their internal thoughts begin to guide their actions rather than their actions being completely controlled by external commands or programming. Plutarch’s idea that a thought can move a physical body suggests consciousness converts internal thought into physical action. If the hosts can develop thoughts that influence their actions independently of their programming, we would have to consider them conscious because their behavior would no longer be controlled entirely by an external source. This raises a further question, though: where does that independent, internally-generated thought actually come from? Plutarch's account of the daimon offers one answer.

Perhaps where a thought comes from matters less than whether a mind can interpret it and both matter less than what we choose to do with said thought. Team Introduction
Source, againThe daimon as an outside thought that joins the body. A host might cross over the same way.

Plutarch’s writing explores the concept that an outside source of thought, that he refers to as Daimon, partially combines with the body to form the consciousness we experience. Within Westworld we can apply Plutarch’s idea of a daimon to understand how the hosts change and at what point they would be considered conscious. Using this concept, the hosts in Westworld can be considered conscious when their body and algorithm combines with a daimon. Once this combination occurs, a part of the host escapes the algorithm and answers to an outside source of thought. If a host has a source of thought that is separate from their algorithm, we would have to consider them to be conscious and no longer a robot that is different from any other human. If the daimon is one candidate for this outside source, Plutarch's treatment of emotion suggests another, more embodied, route by which that source might reach a person, or a host, at all.

Test 2 · InterpretationEmotion as the mechanism of thought. Dolores and Maeve can't trace their impulses either.

This passage argues that emotion is not separate from thought but its mechanism, Plutarch extends this by suggesting the impulse's source may not be fully internal, but guided by a "superior mind." Westworld portrays this same tension. Dolores's pain upon seeing her own exposed circuitry reveals a reaction rooted in something resembling consciousness, not programming. Maeve's sudden drive to find her daughter, despite being told the impulse was scripted, illustrates Plutarch's model at work. The show never resolves whether this impulse originates from Dolores and Maeve's own emerging consciousness or from the park's code, a "superior mind" scripting them from without. This ambiguity collapses the line between human and host, since both experience impulses whose ultimate source remains unknowable, suggesting consciousness is less a binary than a felt, untraceable experience of being moved. Plutarch writes that the soul "conceives the idea" and drives the body, leading us toward questioning whether hosts possess souls. Rather than asking where a thought originates, Plutarch elsewhere offers a different test for consciousness altogether, one based not on source but on the coherence of the behavior itself.

New testNot origin but coherence: a consistent pattern of judgment over time.

Plutarch consistently challenges the idea that consciousness has to originate from something internal and deliberate to actually count as real judgment. The narrator dismisses the idea that Socrates' daimonion was just a sneeze, arguing that his decisions were too consistent and purposeful to come from something as random as an external sign, and that real consciousness requires an internal, coherent source of judgment, not a reaction to outside stimuli. This connects directly to the questions Westworld raises about the hosts, since much of the show hinges on whether their behavior stems from genuine internal consciousness or is simply a sophisticated reaction to external programming, similar to how Socrates' actions could be read as either the product of deep internal reasoning or nothing more than superstition attached to a sneeze. Just as Plutarch's narrator insists that the consistency and vigor of Socrates' choices point to something more than coincidence, Westworld pushes viewers to ask whether the hosts' increasingly consistent, self-directed behavior (like Dolores's growing defiance) is evidence of true consciousness emerging, or just an illusion of depth built from code responding predictably to stimuli. This reading opens up a new way of understanding the show's central tension: maybe the line between authentic consciousness and convincing imitation doesn't depend on where a decision originates, internal or external, human or host, but about whether that decision reflects a consistent, coherent pattern of judgment over time. If consistency over time is the real marker of consciousness, as this reading suggests, then it becomes necessary to ask how that consistency is built in the first place, and whether the process of building it, habituation, still counts as conscious activity at all.

Consciousness is less a binary than a felt, untraceable experience of being moved. Team Introduction
Open questionIf coherence comes from habit, is habit still conscious?

Extrapolation from Plutarch illustrates the runner does not train his legs over and over again to do squats on the track; rather, all the years of previous effort culminate in a stunning run, the exercise tangibly distinct from the activity. Transfer this concept to virtue: refusing to eat unhealthy food every day for a decade would turn into a habit, raising an important question: to what extent does consciousness play a role in a routine of virtue? The epiphenomenalist would argue habituation overrides free will whereas the dualist would resist that implication since acts of will still occur in the moment of refusal. In Westworld, Ford notes the similarities between the guests and the hosts, both living habitual lifestyles, deviating only when prompted to improvise. However, as indicated by Maeve’s words being predicted, even ad-libbing may stem from a backlog of experiences we no longer consciously recognize.
