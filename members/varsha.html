Build 4: Title

In Build 3, I argued that consciousness is inherently narrative because it coordinates past training and present conduct into a meaningful sequence. Epaminondas demonstrates this, his present decisions become intelligible through the history that shaped him. This raises a problem. If narration explains the person Epaminondas becomes, does it explain consciousness itself, or only how Plutarch presents him? Further, if his past explains his present, how much agency does he have over who he becomes?

NoteYour note

But one might object that narration does not fit Plutarch’s conception of consciousness, because it may create the unity it claims to reveal. Epaminondas tells little of his own story; Theanor describes his education under Lysis, while Caphisias narrates his actions during the revolt. It is possible, though the text gives no sign that events are altered, that the continuity between his past and present comes from Plutarch’s arrangement rather than from consciousness itself. Gell describes personhood as something that “can be abducted” from events (222), so readers infer a person from what they are shown. Extending Gell, I think a narrator who selects and connects moments may also build that person. If so, narration may tell us how we understand a person without fully explaining what their said consciousness is. This challenges my original claim: I cannot assume that because Plutarch presents Epaminondas narratively, consciousness itself is inherently narrative.

Yet Plutarch’s portrayal of Epaminondas suggests that narration does more than impose a pattern on separate events. Caphisias reported earlier: he “would not kill any citizen without fair trial, unless under absolute necessity” (112). These connections suggest that his past training genuinely informs his present judgment, even if narration shapes how we recognize that continuity. Narration can reveal how earlier experiences become active in present decisions. This response raises a second objection. If Epaminondas’s present conduct follows from his training, is consciousness merely the product of a past he did not choose? Plutarch’s idea of a conscious mind refers to one that is trained, but who determines what the training should be. He did not choose to be taught by Lysis, and poverty became the household’s “good and loving nurse of youth” (96). Epaminondas himself warns that desires strengthened by long habits under bad education can overpower the soul (98). If our formation shapes how we respond, our actions may be determined by circumstances beyond our control. The Bhagavad Gita pushes this further: Krishna tells Arjuna that action belongs to nature’s qualities, and people think “I am the actor” only when “deluded by individuality” (3.27). If action comes from forces beyond the self, consciousness seems less like an active process and more like the consequence of earlier causes. This highlights Plutarch’s emphasis on Epaminondas’ narrative, by pushing readers to question personal agency and if one truly has any.

NoteYour note

Epaminondas’s own account complicates this view. He describes refusing permitted gain “voluntarily and repeatedly” to train oneself against unlawful gain (99). His circumstances shape him, but he also participates in shaping his character through repeated choices. This does not mean he is entirely free from his past; rather, being formed by earlier experiences does not necessarily make a person passive. His choices help sustain the training that later guides his conduct.

Working through these objections has changed my understanding of narration. I no longer see it simply as the coordination of past and present, but as a way of understanding the relationship between being shaped by one’s history and participating in what comes next. In Section 20, Simmias describes bones and sinews that “lie heavy and inert” until an idea moves them (105). Epaminondas’s repeated refusals shape how he responds to later situations, so his past influences his actions without necessarily determining every future choice. However, if one considers how Plutarch uses narration to show earlier refusals shaping later responses, one can begin to see how narration might allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality to influence its course. It points toward an argument against reductive accounts such as Dennett’s, which explain consciousness through physical processes alone. One question remains: if a sequence is narrated by others, which part of the design is Epaminondas’s own?
